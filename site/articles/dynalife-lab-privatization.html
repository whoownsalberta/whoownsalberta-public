No Documented Business Case, One Bidder, and a $31.5-Million Buyback: Inside Alberta's DynaLIFE Reversal

How ministerial direction drove a $4.8-billion community lab outsourcing commitment without a documented business case—leaving taxpayers with an estimated $77 million in extra costs, a buyout cheque, and restricted audit records.

By WhoOwnsAlberta Investigative DeskRecords verified as of 2026-11-27T08:05:00ZJump to 12 verified receipts ↓

Between 2019 and 2022, Alberta committed to outsourcing routine community laboratory testing across the province under a $4.8-billion, 14-year, four-month services agreement with DynaLIFE [1]. Less than a year after expanded service rolled out, the province reversed course, cancelled the commitment, bought back the contractor's operations for $31.5 million in cash, and absorbed an estimated $77 million in extra public costs [2] [3].

A November 2025 examination by the Auditor General of Alberta, paired with Alberta Health Services (AHS) audited financial statements and January 2022 board minutes, lays bare how that decision happened—and how much of the paper trail remains out of public view [4] [5].

Why some headlines say $109 million: News reports published on November 19, 2025—including Global News carrying Canadian Press reporting—cited roughly $109 million by combining the Auditor General's $77 million extra-cost estimate with the rounded $32 million buyout price ($31.5 million exact in AHS's audited accounts, or $108.5 million unrounded) [7]. The Auditor General's 48-page report never prints $109 million and keeps the asset-and-liability purchase outside its incremental-cost table because AHS acquired tangible operating assets and assumed liabilities at fair value [7]. WhoOwnsAlberta.info reports each accounting basis separately rather than adding them together [7].

Driven from the ministry, signed without a documented business case

In Alberta's health structure during the procurement, AHS ran operations and contracting under a governing board—yet the Auditor General concluded that AHS management and its board were not the strategic decision-makers behind community lab outsourcing [4]. Instead, the examination found that the health minister of the day and the Department of Health expected the outsourcing initiative to move forward from 2019 through 2022 [4].

Crucially, the primary stated goal of handing community lab work to a private operator was cutting costs [8]. Yet when the Auditor General searched for the foundational homework behind a multi-billion-dollar restructuring of essential diagnostic care, the audit uncovered no documented formal business case prepared by either AHS or Alberta Health before procurement began [9].

Then competitive leverage narrowed sharply. While the written request for proposals met internal AHS procurement policy standards, a shortlisted rival—Sonic Healthcare—stepped out of the competition, pointing to pandemic travel barriers that blocked on-the-ground due diligence [10]. An external procurement specialist advised AHS that continuing talks with the single remaining qualified bidder was permissible, leaving Alberta negotiating a provincewide, multi-year deal with just one proponent at the table [10].

One board resolution—and an unchecked condition

Public minutes from January 27, 2022 show how governance moved from policy expectation to binding commitment [11]. At that meeting, the AHS board unanimously authorized AHS and its subsidiary Alberta Precision Laboratories (APL) to enter a term sheet with DynaLIFE, while separately the Auditor General noted the board approved the deal based on its dollar value [11].

There was a crucial guardrail written directly into that January resolution: the board delegated authority to the CEO to sign the final services agreement only if its fundamental final terms did not substantively depart from the term sheet [11].

Four months later, on May 30, 2022, AHS finalized the $4.8-billion services agreement [1]. Because neither the executed term sheet nor the signed contract has been released publicly, Albertans still cannot verify whether the final agreement satisfied the board's condition—or who signed off on the check [11] [6*].

The bill Albertans can see—and the ledger they cannot

When the expanded arrangement ended effective August 31, 2023, AHS brought DynaLIFE's community lab operations back under public delivery through APL, citing the goal of strengthening service for Albertans [1] [8].

Public accounts establish two real financial measures of the reversal—and leave a third glaringly blank:

  • Auditor's extra-cost estimate ($77 million): Comparing roughly eight months of expanded operation against what continuing the previous delivery structure would have cost, the Auditor General estimated approximately $61 million in added net operating cost plus $16 million to bring services back in-house—$77 million across the 2019–2023 initiative [2].
  • Buyout cash consideration ($31.5 million): AHS's audited 2023–24 financial statements disclose $31.5 million in cash consideration paid to acquire DynaLIFE operations on August 31, 2023, calculated after offsetting $16.295 million in net balances DynaLIFE owed AHS. Accounting for cash acquired in the transfer, AHS's consolidated cash-flow statement reports a $29.388 million net cash outflow [3].
  • Gross service payments (undisclosed): Neither the audit nor AHS's financial statements reveal the total operating remittances paid to DynaLIFE during the expanded contract, or which legal entity and ultimate owners received the buyout cash [6*].

Fair Comment & Editorial Analysis

Fair comment: Accountability fails when direction and oversight split apart

Read together, these verified public records reveal a classic governance failure in major health privatization. Strategic momentum came from the political and ministry level, while formal contracting and board approval sat at arm's length inside AHS [4] [11]. Proceeding with a $4.8-billion commitment without a documented business case, after losing competitive tension to a single bidder, placed the downside risk squarely on taxpayers—who ended up absorbing an estimated $77 million in extra costs and paying $31.5 million in cash to buy back essential public lab capacity [9] [10] [2] [3].

Just as troubling is the transparency deficit that followed. When Alberta's independent legislative auditor reports that records access was curtailed with ministry backing, and the public still cannot inspect the executed contract or total vendor payouts, citizens are left without the basic evidence needed to prevent a repeat [5] [6*].

The fight over missing records

The sharpest clash in the public record is not about whether the contract ended—it is about what the Auditor General was allowed to see while examining it.

In the November 2025 report, the Auditor General stated that AHS limited auditors' access to records—and that the Department of Health backed that limitation [5]. Because documents were missing or heavily redacted, the audit carried an explicit scope warning: the full effect of those restrictions on the audit's conclusions could not be determined [9] [5].

Appearing before the Standing Committee on Public Accounts on November 17, 2026, newly appointed Auditor General Phil Peters added further detail: AHS had engaged the legal team that conducted the line-by-line review of more than 10,000 DynaLIFE records, and his office was not encountering the same access dynamics in its ongoing AHS procurement audit, though claims of legal privilege remained an active issue [5].

One year later: two recommendations still outstanding

One full year after the November 2025 examination, both joint Auditor General recommendations—requiring documented analysis before major procurements and complete records showing who made key decisions and why—remained listed as "Not Ready for Assessment" in the November 2026 status trackers for both successor ministries (Hospital and Surgical Health Services and Primary and Preventative Health Services) [12]. Peters confirmed at the November 17, 2026 Public Accounts Committee hearing that both recommendations remain outstanding [12].

Government & Subject Account

The government's account and key audit countercontext

  • Premier Danielle Smith (November 20, 2025, Alberta Hansard): Told the Legislative Assembly that the government cooperated fully with the examination, rejected claims to the contrary, and stated that her government reversed the outsourcing once it became clear DynaLIFE could not run provincewide testing [5] [8].
  • Minister Matt Jones (March 18, 2026, Main Estimates): Acknowledged that the lab transition was not executed to the standard Albertans expected and caused service disruptions and costs, while testifying that the health system is acting on all Auditor General and Wyant recommendations—centralizing procurement under Health Shared Services, commissioning an independent procurement review, and moving APL under Primary Care Alberta [12]. Note that "Not Ready for Assessment" means the Auditor General has not yet been notified that implementation is complete for audit verification, not that zero internal work has begun [12].
  • Initiation timeline: The Auditor General's finding on 2019–2022 ministerial strategic direction covers the pre-Smith period and does not attribute initiation of the outsourcing to Premier Smith [4].
  • Procurement process context: The Auditor General confirmed the written RFP document met internal AHS procurement policy standards, noted Sonic Healthcare cited pandemic travel curbs when withdrawing, and recorded that an independent procurement adviser said AHS could proceed with talks with the lone remaining qualified bidder [10].
  • Scope caveat & Nov. 2026 clarification: Because of the records-access limitation, the audit's no-business-case finding reflects the absence of documented evidence available to auditors rather than absolute proof that no separate internal analysis existed [9]. Nor do available public records establish personal intent behind the access limits; Peters also testified in November 2026 that AHS engaged the legal reviewers and that OAG is not seeing the same access dynamics in its ongoing AHS procurement audit [5].

Missing Records & Open Questions

What Albertans still have not been shown

Even after a full Auditor General examination and two years of audited AHS financial statements, four decisive public-interest records remain missing from the public record [6*]:

  1. The executed May 30, 2022 Services Agreement, any subsequent amendments, and board-condition sign-off confirming whether final terms matched the January 27, 2022 term sheet [11] [6*].
  2. A reconciled AHS/APL service-payment remittance ledger establishing gross operating cash paid to DynaLIFE and any withheld invoices or credits [6*].
  3. The August 31, 2023 closing settlement statement identifying the exact legal cash payee of the $31.5 million acquisition consideration and how the $16.295 million offset was calculated [3] [6*].
  4. Certified historical Alberta Corporate Registry partnership records establishing exact contracting partners and ownership interests at signing and exit [6*].
