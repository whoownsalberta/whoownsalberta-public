What Bill 44 Actually Changes: Police Control, Custody Inquiries, Your Medical Records, and the $200M Tab

A plain-English guide to Alberta's 163-page omnibus bill: moving the Sheriffs Police onto the government payroll, ending automatic public inquiries into deaths in custody, sharing health records without consent, and ramping policing budgets without a ballot question.

By WhoOwnsAlberta Investigative DeskPublished: 2026-11-27Records verified as of 2026-11-27T21:31:00ZJump to 13 verified receipts ↓

On November 23, 2026, Alberta's Justice Minister introduced Bill 44—a 163-page omnibus bill that rewrites 20 different provincial laws at once [1] [2].

If you only saw the headlines, you might think it was mostly about voter-data protections and road-safety rules [2]. Look inside the actual bill, however, and three much bigger changes jump out—affecting how Alberta's new provincial police force is run, whether deaths in police custody still trigger automatic public inquiries, and whether your identifiable medical records can be shared across government without your consent [1] [3] [4].

We checked the full bill text against Alberta's provincial budgets, Cabinet orders, the government's own Alberta Next Panel report, and the policing and inquest laws in Ontario, Quebec, British Columbia, and Manitoba [5] [6]. Here is what Bill 44 actually changes, what it leaves untouched, and what Albertans still haven't been shown [7] [8*].

1. Alberta's new police force is moving onto the government payroll

Less than two years ago, Premier Danielle Smith's government set up the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service as an arm's-length Crown corporation—with its own corporate board of directors and separate legal standing from the government ministry [1] [11].

Since then, the province has steadily dismantled that separation:

  • July 2025: Cabinet passed a regulation explicitly barring members of the civilian Oversight Board from also sitting on the police service's corporate board of directors [12].
  • April 2026: Cabinet quietly repealed that rule, allowing the same appointees to sit on both boards [12].
  • November 2026 (Bill 44): The government now proposes scrapping the Crown corporation altogether. Officers and staff would be employed directly under Alberta's Public Service Act, municipal policing contracts would move directly to the provincial Crown, and the old corporation would wind down over three years [1].

What stays protected: To be clear, Bill 44 does not let politicians order police who to arrest or how to run criminal cases [7]. The law still strictly bars the Minister from interfering in specific investigations, operations, officer discipline, or day-to-day administration [7]. The civilian Oversight Board and independent Police Review Commission remain in place, the Crown takes on explicit legal liability for officers' actions on the job, and housing a provincial police force inside a ministry is the same basic setup used by Ontario's OPP and Quebec's Sûreté du Québec [7] [6].

2. Automatic public inquiries into deaths in custody would end

Where Bill 44 makes its sharpest break with Western Canada and Ontario is over what happens when someone dies in police custody, from police use of force, or inside a jail [3] [6].

  • Before Bill 44: Under a rule codified in 2005 (and narrowed under the Notley NDP in 2017 only to avoid duplicating Child and Youth Advocate reviews of youth deaths), Alberta's Fatality Review Board must recommend a public fatality inquiry for deaths in police or jail custody unless the death was clearly natural and unpreventable or had no meaningful tie to care or supervision [3]. British Columbia, Manitoba, and Ontario all keep mandatory custodial inquests too—and in BC, even when a coroner waives an inquest under a natural-death exception, the law forces them to publish their reasons [6].
  • Under Bill 44: That automatic guarantee is repealed [3]. Instead, the Fatality Review Board would decide whether a public inquiry is warranted using "prescribed factors" written later by Cabinet behind closed doors—rules that have not yet been published [3] [8*]. If the Board says no, the matter ends there, with no legal requirement to publish why [3].

Families would also lose a clear right to records: getting a Chief Medical Examiner's report would shift from something the examiner must provide next of kin to something they may release under Cabinet rules and fees [3].

Two other gaps stand out in the text. First, Bill 44 requires deaths inside Alberta's upcoming involuntary addiction-treatment facilities (enacted in 2025 under the Compassionate Intervention Act) to be reported to a medical examiner, but does not guarantee public fatality inquiries into those deaths [3] [11]. Second, unlike the policing section—which gives the old corporation a three-year wind-down—the fatality-inquiry section includes no transitional clause explaining what happens to pending inquiries already waiting for a hearing [3] [8*]. News reports attribute a backlog of 180 pending inquiries (125 mandatory and 55 discretionary) to Alberta Justice, though no primary public docket confirming those exact counts has been released [8*].

3. Your medical records could be linked across ministries without asking you

Tucked deep into the same bill is a major change to Alberta's data-privacy rules [4].

Under Bill 44, for minister-approved public-sector analytics projects, health custodians could hand over individually identifying health records without asking the patient's consent [4]. If a health organization objects, a minister-designated analytics director could issue a binding order forcing disclosure anyway [4].

Instead of requiring a separate privacy check for each project, one blanket Privacy Impact Assessment would cover the whole program—and Cabinet would gain the power to override additional sections of Alberta's Health Information Act later by regulation [4]. The bill does keep guardrails: those blanket assessments and annual updates must be filed with Alberta's Information and Privacy Commissioner, re-identifying people from linked datasets remains restricted, and annual public reports are required [4].

4. Rising policing bills, an outdated cost study, and a missing ballot question

Follow the money in Alberta's official budgets, and two trends stand out side by side:

  • The provincial Sheriffs budget is growing fast: Voted funding for the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service climbed from $136.7 million (2024–25) and $156.0 million (original 2025–26 budget, restated to $164.1 million after an $8.0 million internal shift from Law Enforcement Oversight) to $200.9 million in 2026–27—including $36.9 million specifically for expanding the provincial police service [5]. Combined with Contract Policing and Oversight ($432.3 million), those two policing lines reached $633.2 million this year, alongside separate allocations of $14.9 million for border patrol teams and $26.4 million for the Police Review Commission [5].
  • Rural municipalities are paying a bigger share: Under the Renewed Police Funding Model that started April 1, 2026, rural and small-urban municipalities pay 22% of frontline RCMP costs this year, stepping up two percentage points every year until they hit 30% in 2030–31 [9].
  • Still no updated price tag: Five years after the 2021 PwC study under Jason Kenney estimated ~$366 million in six-year transition costs and $735M–$759M in annual operating costs (plus roughly $170 million a year in federal RCMP cost-sharing at stake), the province has not published a single updated transition cost study [9] [10*].

Meanwhile, Albertans never got a direct vote on policing. In December 2025, the government's own Alberta Next Panel reported 52% opposition to replacing the RCMP in professional polling (with support sitting at 44%, 41%, and 48% across three 2025 surveys—the lowest of all seven proposals tested) and majority opposition in online surveys [13]. The panel still advised moving ahead without a referendum, arguing more than 80% of Albertans are already policed by city forces or sheriffs [13]. When Cabinet put ten questions on the October 19, 2026 provincial referendum ballot, policing wasn't one of them [13].

Fair Comment & Editorial Analysis

What this adds up to (Fair Comment)

Taken one piece at a time, several parts of Bill 44 have ordinary explanations. Running a provincial police service inside a ministry is how Ontario and Quebec already do it, and clearing a multi-year backlog of fatality inquiries is a real problem worth solving [6] [2].

Put the pieces together, however, and a clear pattern emerges across six years of Alberta laws: police, detention, and data-collection powers are being expanded in open legislation, while public accountability rules—automatic inquiries into deaths in custody, families' right to medical-examiner reports, and privacy override limits—are being shifted out of open law and into unpublished Cabinet regulations [3] [4] [11].

At the same time, Albertans are watching Sheriffs Police budgets cross $200 million and rural policing bills climb toward 30% without an updated cost study—and without a ballot question, even after the government's own panel found most Albertans polled opposed replacing the RCMP [5] [9] [13] [10*].

Government & Subject Account

What the government says—and what stays protected

  • Justice Minister Mickey Amery (Nov. 23, 2026, Hansard): Said Bill 44 protects Alberta's voter list, modernizes law-enforcement laws, improves SafeRoads Alberta, and helps grieving families get faster, more accurate answers after sudden or unexpected deaths [2].
  • Public Safety Minister Mike Ellis (Nov. 25, 2026, Hansard): Said rising municipal policing bills come directly from rising RCMP costs paid by towns to the RCMP, and maintained that the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service supports and augments—rather than immediately replaces—the RCMP and city police forces [2].
  • RCMP staffing & the 2032 federal contract (Alberta Next Panel, Dec. 2025): Pointed to roughly 500 unfilled positions out of 3,151 contracted Alberta RCMP officer spots, plus a March 2025 federal white paper proposing to reorganize the RCMP and exit contract policing around 2032 [2].
  • Safeguards kept in place: Politicians remain barred by law from interfering in specific police investigations, operations, or officer discipline; the civilian Oversight Board, Police Review Commission, and independent serious-incident unit stay intact with expanded complaint reporting; the Crown takes on explicit vicarious liability; medical examiners gain stronger court-backed power to demand records and mandatory notice of deaths in involuntary addiction facilities; and cross-ministry data analytics still requires Privacy Commissioner filings, re-identification limits, and annual public reports [7] [3] [4].
  • Other provinces & rural offsets: Ontario and Quebec both house provincial police inside ministries; Quebec uses discretionary public inquests paired with mandatory independent police-death investigations (BEI); and Alberta's Renewed Police Funding Model added a Vacancy Modifier and Population Density Subsidy to cushion rural costs [6] [9].
