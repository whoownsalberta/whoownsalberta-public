Inside Bill 44: Alberta's Provincial Police Reversal, Custodial Death Inquiries, and $200M Sheriffs Budget

Primary statutory text and Government Estimates separate what Alberta's 163-page First Reading omnibus bill (168-page PDF amending 20 statutes) changes—from Crown-corp repeal to ending mandatory in-custody fatality inquiries and non-consensual health data linking—from the oversight guardrails it leaves intact.

By WhoOwnsAlberta Research DeskPublished: 2026-11-27Records verified as of 2026-11-27T21:31:00ZJump to 13 verified receipts ↓

On November 23, 2026, Alberta Justice Minister Mickey Amery tabled Bill 44 (Justice, Public Safety and Information Statutes Amendment Act, 2026)—a 163-page First Reading omnibus bill (168-page PDF) amending 20 provincial laws [1] [2]. Paired alongside voter-data safeguards and SafeRoads changes are three consequential structural shifts: dissolving the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service's arm's-length Crown-corporation status less than two years after legislating it, repealing mandatory public fatality inquiries into deaths in police and prison custody, and authorizing non-consensual cross-ministry linking of identifiable health records [1] [3] [4] [2].

Reconciling the primary bill text against Alberta's 2025–26 and 2026–27 Government Estimates, Cabinet Orders in Council, the December 2025 Alberta Next Panel report, prior Alberta amendments, and verified primary statutes in Ontario, Quebec, British Columbia, and Manitoba separates what Bill 44 actually changes from the guardrails it leaves intact—and exposes the records Albertans still have not been shown [5] [6] [7].

From Crown corporation to direct government payroll in eighteen months

Alberta's new provincial police structure did not arrive in a single vote. Across three consecutive legislative sessions under Premier Danielle Smith, the government built—and then structurally reversed—the legal wrapper around the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service (ASPS) [1] [10].

First came Bill 11 (Public Safety Statutes Amendment Act, 2024), inserting an initial Part 3.2 framework and civilian Oversight Board into the Police Act [10]. Next came Bill 49 (Public Safety and Emergency Services Statutes Amendment Act, 2025), which formally designated independent agency police services as separate Crown corporations with natural-person legal powers, independent boards of directors, and separate corporate financial authority [1] [10]. In spring 2026, Bill 15 added elaborate employee-transfer and successor-bargaining chapters (Parts 3.3 and 3.4) to move Crown employees into that separate corporate employer, while also allowing delegation of Safer Communities and Neighbourhoods Act (SCAN)—a 2007 PC-era civil property-enforcement statute—to ASPS [10].

Then the corporate firewall began coming down. On April 16, 2026, Cabinet issued Order in Council 116/2026 (AR 72/2026), repealing section 3(3)(a) of the Independent Agency Police Service Regulation (AR 163/2025)—the clause that had kept members of the Oversight Board off the corporate board of directors [11]. Seven months later, Bill 44 proposes repealing the Crown corporation altogether: ASPS becomes a non-corporate statutory police service staffed directly through the Public Service Act, municipal policing agreements move directly to the Crown in right of Alberta, the Bill 15 employee-transfer chapters are scrapped, and the existing corporation enters a three-year wind-down window [1].

Crucially, however, primary statutory text contradicts any claim that Bill 44 places criminal investigations under political command. **Section 33.94(2) of the Police Act stays fully intact**: the Minister remains barred by statute from directing any ASPS officer (including the chief) or issuing policies or plans governing specific investigations, specific operations, individual officer discipline, or day-to-day administration [5]. The minister-appointed civilian Oversight Board (s. 33.8), the Police Review Commission, and the s. 43.3 integrated investigative unit all remain—and Bill 44 codifies explicit Crown vicarious liability (s. 40.1) and expands mandatory misconduct reporting to the oversight commission [5]. Nor is non-corporate ministerial housing unusual in Canada: under Ontario's Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 and Quebec's Police Act (s. 50), both the OPP and the Sûreté du Québec operate under provincial ministries rather than separate Crown corporations [7].

Ending mandatory public inquiries into deaths in custody

Where Bill 44 breaks most sharply with Western Canadian and Ontario statutory guarantees is Section 6—its rewrite of Alberta's Fatality Inquiries Act [3] [7].

Under Alberta's existing s. 33(3) (codified under the PC government in 2005, and narrowed under the Notley NDP in 2017 only to carve out youth deaths already reviewed by the Child and Youth Advocate under s. 33(3.1)–(3.2) while keeping adult custody inquiries mandatory), whenever someone dies in peace-officer custody, from an on-duty peace officer's use of force, or inside a correctional institution, the Fatality Review Board must recommend a public fatality inquiry unless satisfied that the death resulted solely from natural, unpreventable causes or had no meaningful connection to care or supervision [3]. That statutory default parallels British Columbia (Coroners Act, s. 18(2)–(2.1)), Manitoba (The Fatality Inquiries Act, s. 19(5)), and Ontario (Coroners Act, s. 10(4)) [7]. In British Columbia, even if the chief coroner waives a custodial inquest under a narrow natural-death exception, the statute requires reporting the reasons to the minister and publishing them (whereas Quebec pairs discretionary public inquests under Coroners Act ss. 104–106 with mandatory independent BEI police-death investigations) [7].

Bill 44 repeals s. 33(3) outright [3]. In its place, both the Chief Medical Examiner (for certain unestablished or child-in-custody deaths) and the Fatality Review Board would decide whether an inquiry is warranted by applying "prescribed factors" to be written later by Cabinet regulation—criteria that have not yet been published [3] [13*]. If the Board concludes an inquiry is not warranted, it takes no further action, with no statutory duty to publish reasons [3]. At the same time, Bill 44 downgrades families' right to obtain Chief Medical Examiner reports from a mandatory statutory entitlement (shall) to a discretionary regulation-controlled release (may) subject to prescribed classes and fees [3].

Two further details stand out directly in the bill text:

  • Involuntary addiction treatment centres: Bill 44 adds compulsory detention facilities created under the 2025 Compassionate Intervention Act to mandatory medical-examiner notification (s. 12(1)(a)), ensuring death investigations occur, but without restoring automatic public fatality inquiries [3] [10].
  • Missing transitional protection: While Bill 44's Police Act section includes a three-year transitional wind-down clause, Section 6 contains no transitional savings clause clarifying what happens to pending mandatory inquiries once the repeal is proclaimed [3] [13*]. Secondary news reports cite 180 pending inquiries (125 mandatory and 55 discretionary) with multi-year delays attributed to Alberta Justice, though no primary public court docket confirming those exact counts has yet been published [13*].

Health records without consent, and policing bills without a ballot

A third statutory change inside Bill 44 has received far less attention: Section 10 rewrites the Office of Statistics and Information Act to create a new cross-government Public Sector Data Analytics regime [4]. For minister-approved "areas of interest," health custodians may disclose individually identifying health information without individual consent (s. 8.031(2)), a minister-designated director can authorize binding disclosure orders overriding custodian objections (ss. 8.05–8.051), one blanket comprehensive privacy impact assessment replaces project-by-project PIAs under POPA and the Health Information Act (s. 8.061(3)), and Cabinet gains regulation-making power to prescribe additional Health Information Act sections overridden by the analytics regime (ss. 8.071, 11(m)) [4]. The bill pairs those powers with OIPC PIA filings and annual updates, POPA re-identification limits, and public annual reporting [4].

Meanwhile, the policing budget and democratic-mandate records reveal a widening gap between spending growth and public consent:

  • Reconciled Estimates: In Alberta's 2025–26 and 2026–27 Government Estimates, Program 2.1 (renamed Alberta Sheriffs Police Service Funding) climbed from $136.738M (2024–25 Budget) and $156.035M (original 2025–26 Estimate; restated $164.066M Comparable Budget following an intra-ministry +$8.031M reclassification from Law Enforcement Oversight alongside splitting out the Police Review Commission) to $200.942M (2026–27 Estimate, including +$36.9M for ASPS implementation) [6]. Combined with Contract Policing and Policing Oversight ($397.192M → $432.289M), the two voted lines rose from $553.227M (original 2025–26 Estimates; $561.258M restated) to $633.231M in 2026–27 [6]. Separate 2026–27 allocations direct $14.9M to Sheriffs Interdiction Patrol Teams and $26.4M to the Police Review Commission [6].
  • Municipal cost-shift and missing updated costing: Under the Renewed Police Funding Model taking effect April 1, 2026, rural and small-urban municipalities pay 22% of actual frontline RCMP costs in 2026–27, escalating annually to 30% by 2030–31+ [8]. Yet five years after the Kenney-era 2021 PwC study forecast ~$366M in six-year transition costs and $735M–$759M in annual operating costs (plus potential loss of ~$170M in federal cost-sharing), the province has published no updated transition cost study [8] [9*].
  • No referendum despite the panel's own polling: In December 2025, the government's own Alberta Next Panel reported 52% opposition to replacing the RCMP in professional polling (44%, 41%, and 48% support across three 2025 waves—the lowest support of all seven sovereignty proposals tested) and majority opposition in online surveys [12]. The panel nonetheless recommended transitioning community policing to an Alberta Police Service without a referendum, arguing that more than 80% of Albertans are already policed by municipal services or sheriffs [12]. When Cabinet issued Orders in Council 109/2026, 110/2026, and 160/2026 setting ten questions for the October 19, 2026 referendum, policing was omitted completely [12].

Fair Comment & Editorial Analysis

Fair comment: Structural transparency is moving from statute to Cabinet discretion

Taken separately, several elements of Bill 44 have conventional administrative rationales—housing a provincial police force inside a ministry matches Ontario and Quebec, and clearing a multi-year fatality-inquiry backlog is a genuine public-administration problem [7] [2]. Taken together across six years of statutes, however, a consistent governance pattern emerges: expanding enforcement and data-collection reach through primary legislation while shifting public accountability triggers—mandatory custodial fatality inquiries, family report rights, and Health Information Act override boundaries—out of open statutory guarantees and into unpublished Cabinet regulations [3] [4] [10].

Similarly, ramping ASPS operating budgets past $200 million and raising rural municipal policing shares toward 30% without publishing a current transition cost study—and while excluding policing from a ten-question provincial referendum after the government's own panel logged majority polling opposition—asks Alberta taxpayers to fund an institutional build-out whose full price tag and democratic mandate remain unestablished [6] [8] [12] [9*].

Government & Subject Account

The government's account, retained safeguards, and countercontext

  • Justice Minister Mickey Amery (November 23, 2026, Hansard): Introduced Bill 44 as legislation to protect Alberta's list of electors, modernize law-enforcement statutes, improve road safety under SafeRoads Alberta, and help grieving families obtain timely and accurate answers after sudden or unexpected deaths [2].
  • Public Safety Minister Mike Ellis (November 25, 2026, Hansard): Stated that rising municipal policing bills stem directly from rising RCMP costs paid by municipalities to the RCMP, and maintained that the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service augments and supports—rather than immediately replaces—the RCMP, Calgary Police Service, and Edmonton Police Service [2].
  • Federal 2032 RCMP uncertainty & vacancies (Alberta Next Panel, December 2025): Cited roughly 500 unfilled positions among 3,151 contracted Alberta RCMP officer positions and the federal government's March 10, 2025 white paper proposing to reorganize the RCMP and exit contract policing around the 2032 PPSA expiry [2].
  • Statutory safeguards retained by Bill 44: Operational independence (Police Act s. 33.94(2)) barring ministerial interference in specific investigations, operations, or discipline remains unchanged; the civilian Oversight Board (s. 33.8), Police Review Commission, and s. 43.3 integrated investigative unit remain intact and receive expanded reporting mandates; Crown vicarious liability (s. 40.1) is codified; medical examiners gain new court-enforceable records powers (FIA s. 21.1) and mandatory notice of deaths in compulsory addiction facilities (s. 12(1)(a)); and cross-ministry analytics remains subject to OIPC PIA filings, POPA re-identification prohibitions, and public annual reporting [5] [3] [4].
  • Interjurisdictional & formula context: Non-corporate provincial police forces operate under provincial ministries in Ontario and Quebec; Quebec uses discretionary public inquests alongside mandatory independent BEI investigations; and Alberta's Renewed Police Funding Model introduced a Vacancy Modifier and Population Density Subsidy to offset rural costs [7] [8].
