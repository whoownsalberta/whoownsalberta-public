Provincial Police Governance & Retained Safeguards

Reconciles Bill 44 Section 12 and OIC 116/2026 against the Police Act's 2024-25 Crown-corporation framework and retained statutory non-interference and oversight protections.

Bill 44 repeals the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service's Crown-corporation status and moves staff under the Public Service Act

Evidence status: documented.

Introduced on November 23, 2026 under Premier Danielle Smith as a 163-page First Reading omnibus bill (168-page PDF amending 20 provincial statutes), Section 12 of Bill 44 repeals the Police Act's 2025 Crown-corporation structure (s. 33.4(2)) and the Bill 15 employee-transfer chapters (Parts 3.3 and 3.4). It establishes the Alberta Sheriffs Police Service directly as a non-corporate statutory force whose employees are appointed under the Public Service Act, shifts municipal service contracts under s. 33.75 to the Crown in right of Alberta, and provides a three-year transitional wind-down window for the existing corporation (s. 33.951).

Bill 44 retains the civilian Oversight Board, statutory ministerial non-interference in operations, and Police Review Commission oversight

Evidence status: documented.

While removing corporate status, Bill 44 leaves intact Police Act s. 33.8 (the minister-appointed Independent Agency Police Service Oversight Board, barring MLAs and ASPS officers) and s. 33.94(2) (prohibiting the Minister from directing any ASPS officer including the chief or issuing policies respecting specific investigations, operations, discipline, or day-to-day administration). Bill 44 also codifies Crown vicarious liability (s. 40.1), adds chief accountability to report related Level 4/5 complaint dispositions to the Police Review Commission (s. 33.7(3.1)), and expands mandatory referrals under the Peace Officer Act.

April 16, 2026 Order in Council 116/2026 repealed the regulatory rule separating corporate directors from the Oversight Board

Evidence status: documented.

On July 2, 2025, Order in Council 241/2025 enacted AR 163/2025, whose section 3(3)(a) barred members of the Independent Agency Police Service Oversight Board from serving concurrently on the corporate board of directors. Nine months later, on April 16, 2026, Cabinet issued Order in Council 116/2026 (AR 72/2026) repealing section 3(3)(a) to remove that board-overlap bar—seven months before Bill 44 proposed abolishing the corporate structure altogether.

2020-2026 enforcement, detention, and data statutes span both Kenney and Smith governments plus a 2007 PC origin

Evidence status: documented.

Primary statutes show a multi-premier expansion of provincial enforcement and detention authorities: under Premier Jason Kenney, Bill 1 (2020) enacted the Critical Infrastructure Defence Act (CIDA); under Premier Danielle Smith, Bill 11 (2024) and Bill 49 (2025) created the independent agency police framework, Bill 31 (2024) and Bill 45 (2025) expanded CIDA to a two-kilometre U.S. border corridor and energy facilities, Bill 53 (2025) enacted the Compassionate Intervention Act authorizing involuntary addiction detention, Bill 4 (2025) expanded Clare's Law collection and disclosure of health and personal records for threat assessments, and Bill 15 (2026) authorized delegating Safer Communities and Neighbourhoods Act (originally enacted in 2007 under PC Premier Ed Stelmach) powers to ASPS.

Fatality Inquiries & Cross-Ministry Health Data Linking

Before-and-after text comparison of Bill 44 Sections 6 and 10 alongside interjurisdictional statutory baselines and unverified backlog/regulation gaps.

Bill 44 repeals mandatory public fatality inquiries for custodial deaths, shifts next-of-kin report access to regulation, and omits a transitional clause

Evidence status: documented.

Section 6 of Bill 44 repeals Fatality Inquiries Act s. 33(3)—codified in 2005 (PC era) and narrowed in 2017 (Notley NDP, SA 2017 c 8 s 2) only to carve out youth deaths already reviewed by the Child and Youth Advocate (s. 33(3.1)-(3.2)) while keeping adult peace-officer and correctional custody inquiries mandatory unless due solely to natural, unpreventable causes or unrelated to care. In its place, the Board applies unpublished Cabinet-prescribed factors (ss. 33(2), 55(e)) with no statutory duty to publish reasons when declining an inquiry. Section 6 also downgrades next-of-kin medical-examiner report access to discretionary under regulation (s. 30(2)), adds compulsory addiction facilities to medical-examiner notification only (s. 12(1)(a)), and contains no transitional savings clause for pending inquiries.

Bill 44 authorizes cross-ministry linking of identifiable health records without consent and allows Cabinet to expand Health Information Act overrides

Evidence status: documented.

Section 10 of Bill 44 adds Part 3 (Public Sector Data Analytics) to the Office of Statistics and Information Act, permitting eligible health custodians to disclose individually identifying health information without individual consent (s. 8.031(2)) and empowering a minister-designated director to authorize binding disclosure orders over custodian objections (ss. 8.05-8.051). A single comprehensive privacy impact assessment replaces project-level PIAs under POPA and the Health Information Act (s. 8.061(3)), and Part 3 prevails over specified Health Information Act sections plus any additional sections prescribed by Cabinet regulation (ss. 8.071, 11(m)), while retaining OIPC PIA filings, POPA re-identification limits, and public annual reporting.

Four-province statutory baseline: ON and QC house police under ministries, while BC, MB, and ON mandate custodial inquests

Evidence status: documented.

Verified primary statutes across four Canadian provinces distinguish policing structure from custodial-death inquests. On policing, Ontario's Community Safety and Policing Act, 2019 (s. 2(1)) employs OPP officers under Ontario's Public Service Act and Quebec's Police Act (s. 50) places the Sûreté du Québec under the Minister of Public Security—both non-corporate ministry models. On custodial deaths, however, British Columbia (Coroners Act, s. 18(2)-(2.1)), Manitoba (The Fatality Inquiries Act, s. 19(5)), and Ontario (Coroners Act, s. 10(4)) all mandate public inquests for custodial or peace-officer force deaths (with BC obligating published reasons whenever waived), whereas Quebec pairs discretionary inquests (Coroners Act, ss. 104-106) with mandatory independent BEI police-death investigations (Police Act, ss. 289.1-289.2).
